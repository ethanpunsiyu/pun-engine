A good pun is a complete utterance that carries actual meaning inside its
surrounding discourse AND simultaneously activates two readings of an
ambiguous pivot word. Both readings should be true at the same time.

Required properties:

1. Phonetic closeness — the pivot must sound like (or be identical to)
   the word that anchors the second reading. Loose homophones are weaker
   than exact ones.

2. Ambiguity — both readings of the pivot must be plausible inside the
   sentence at the same time, not one obvious reading with a forced
   second one bolted on.

3. Distinctiveness — each reading must be anchored by its own balanced
   set of surrounding context words. If both readings lean on the same
   evidence, the pun collapses.

4. Surprise — the pivot should be unexpected at its local slot while
   remaining sensible inside the global topic. The second reading lands
   as a small reveal, not an obvious telegraph.

5. Discourse fit — the utterance must say something coherent about the
   topic for its intended audience. An off-topic remark, however clever
   the wordplay, is a poor pun.

Avoid:
  - pivots that do not actually sound alike;
  - readings that fail to fit grammatically into the sentence;
  - "joke wrapper" sentences that exist only to deliver the pun and
    contribute nothing else;
  - puns that mock or alienate the stated audience;
  - relying on rare, archaic, or obscure word senses the audience would
    not recognise.

Tone shaping: the tone parameter governs register, not content. A
"dry" or "deadpan" pun still satisfies all five properties; a "playful"
one does too. Tone affects sentence rhythm and word choice, never the
underlying mechanics.
